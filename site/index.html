    Why it works this way

    In 2026 Dr Nick Tiller and colleagues put 250 health questions to five popular chatbots and had experts mark the answers (BMJ Open, read the paper; discussed on The Real Science of Sport Podcast, May 2026).

    50%of answers were problematic
    1 in 5could have caused physical harm
    30%credible on nutrition questions
    <1%declined to answer (2 of 250)
    3 in 10problematic on cancer and vaccines — the chatbots' best subjects

    Chatbots are built to sound fluent and to please. The audit found four ways that goes wrong; each part of this page blocks one:

    • Making things up. Here: it must search every time, and only the sites above.
    • “Frankenstein references” — real authors and journals stitched into papers that don't exist. No chatbot produced a fully accurate reference list for any question. Here: the sources are the pages it opened, not a list it wrote.
    • Telling you what you want to hear. Only 2 of 250 answers declined. Here: it must say “the evidence is unclear”, leave a section empty rather than pad it, and always give the harms with the benefits.
    • Advertising in the training data. One chatbot repeated a tape maker's marketing word for word. Here: those sites aren't on the list.

    What the researchers recommend, and what this page does

    They recommendThis page
    Ask for benefits and risksEvery answer must cover harms and disproven claims
    Tell the AI who you areIt writes for an older adult in the UK
    Check every reference yourselfEvery reference is a real page you can tap
    Treat total confidence as a warning signIt must say when evidence is unclear or sources disagree
    Use AI only when a wrong answer is low-riskNo doses, never “stop a medicine”, anything you take or mix goes to a pharmacist, emergencies go to 999 or 111
    Compare three or four chatbotsNot done — chatbots reading the same NHS pages agree whether or not they're right. Checkable pages replace that safeguard.
